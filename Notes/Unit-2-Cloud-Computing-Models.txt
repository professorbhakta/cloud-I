0. BASIC TERMINOLOGIES (FOUNDATION)
================================================================================

Unit 2 is about models — that is, structured ways of classifying how cloud is
delivered, where it runs, and how it is designed. A few terms should be clear
before entering the detailed chapters.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
0.1 CLOUD SERVICE MODEL
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A cloud service model classifies cloud offerings according to the kind of
capability delivered and, equally important, according to the split of management
responsibility between provider and customer. The classical service models are
Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS), and Software as
a Service (SaaS). The important factor is the trade-off between control and
convenience: lower layers give more control but demand more administration, while
higher layers are easier to use but offer less deep customization.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
0.2 CLOUD DEPLOYMENT MODEL OR TYPE OF CLOUD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A deployment model classifies cloud environments by ownership, access scope, and
tenancy pattern. The standard types are public, private, hybrid, and community
cloud. Here the important factor is not “what software feature is offered,” but
“who owns or exclusively uses the environment, and how is it shared.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------
0.3 SPI STACK AND SHARED RESPONSIBILITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------

SPI stands for Software, Platform, and Infrastructure as a Service. It is often
visualized as a stack in which infrastructure sits at the bottom, platform in the
middle, and software at the top. Shared responsibility means security and
operations duties are divided: the provider secures the cloud foundation, while
the customer secures identities, data, and configurations appropriate to the
chosen model. Misunderstanding this split is a common cause of both exam mistakes
and real incidents.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
0.4 CLOUD ARCHITECTURE AND REFERENCE MODEL
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cloud architecture refers to the structural arrangement of components such as
clients, applications, services, runtime, storage, infrastructure, management,
and security. A reference model is a conceptual framework that standardizes how
layers and roles relate, helping students and architects discuss different
provider systems using a common language.


================================================================================
1. OVERVIEW — TYPES OF CLOUD AND CLOUD COMPUTING MODELS
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.1 WHAT THIS UNIT REALLY STUDIES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cloud computing models answer three practical questions. First, how is the
service delivered to the consumer — as raw infrastructure, as a development
platform, or as finished software? Second, where is the cloud environment
deployed and who is allowed to use it — the general public, a single
organization, a community, or a mixture? Third, how are the internal pieces
organized — that is, what architecture and reference layers explain the system?

Unit 2 therefore connects business choices with technical structure. Choosing
SaaS for email is not only a product decision; it is a service-model decision.
Keeping patient records in a private cloud is not only a security preference; it
is a deployment-model decision. Designing front-end and back-end components is an
architecture decision. All three perspectives belong together.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.2 WHY MODELS MATTER
------------------------------------------------------------------------

If the wrong service model is chosen, a team may either drown in server
administration or feel trapped by insufficient customization. If the wrong
deployment model is chosen, an organization may overspend on private capacity or
take unacceptable compliance risk on public infrastructure. The important factors
in every model question are cost, control, security, compliance, skill
requirements, and speed of delivery.

A simple real-life illustration is a student project. Building every layer on
IaaS virtual machines can teach internals deeply, but for a short deadline a PaaS
or SaaS approach may deliver a usable result faster. Model choice is therefore
contextual.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.3 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Begin long answers by stating that cloud models are mainly discussed as service
models and deployment models, then architecture and reference model where asked.
This opening shows syllabus awareness immediately.

2. CLOUD SERVICE MODELS (SPI STACK)
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1 MEANING OF SERVICE MODELS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cloud service models describe categories of cloud capability and the management
boundary between provider and customer. They are called service models because
the customer consumes a service rather than merely buying a box of hardware. The
same underlying data center may support all three SPI models at once; what changes
is how much of the stack is abstracted away from the consumer.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2 THE SPI STACK IN CONTEXT
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Imagine a stack. At the bottom, IaaS offers virtual machines, storage, and
networks. In the middle, PaaS offers an application platform with runtime and
deployment tools. At the top, SaaS offers finished applications ready for end
users. As one moves upward, the provider manages more, and the customer focuses
on narrower concerns. As one moves downward, the customer gains freedom to
configure operating systems and middleware, but also inherits more responsibility.

A popular teaching analogy is pizza as a service. With IaaS, you rent the kitchen
equipment and cook yourself. With PaaS, many ingredients and tools are prepared,
so you concentrate on finishing the dish. With SaaS, you simply dine — the meal
is served. The analogy is useful in exams if followed by technical explanation,
not used alone.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3 IMPORTANT FACTORS COMMON TO ALL SERVICE MODELS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Across IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS, several factors should be discussed. Who manages the
operating system, runtime, application, and data? How quickly can a team start
producing value? How deep is customization? What skills are required? How strong
is the risk of vendor lock-in? How is billing calculated — by virtual-machine
hours, platform usage, or per-user subscription? Good answers explicitly weigh
these factors rather than declaring one model universally best.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Whenever a question says “explain service models,” define the SPI stack first,
then dedicate separate elaborated paragraphs to IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS, and end
with a comparison.

3. INFRASTRUCTURE AS A SERVICE (IaaS)
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1 DEFINITION AND CORE IDEA
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Infrastructure as a Service provides fundamental computing resources over the
Internet, especially virtual machines, storage, and networking. Instead of buying
physical servers, an organization rents virtual infrastructure and configures it
according to need. IaaS is the cloud model closest to traditional system
administration because the customer still thinks in terms of machines, disks,
operating systems, and network rules, even though the physical hardware remains
with the provider.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2 RESPONSIBILITY SPLIT
------------------------------------------------------------------------

In a typical IaaS arrangement, the provider manages the data center facility,
physical servers, storage hardware, core networking gear, and the virtualization
layer that makes virtual machines possible. The customer manages the guest
operating system, middleware, runtime, applications, and data. The customer also
usually controls virtual network settings such as firewall rules and subnetting
inside the provisioned virtual network.

This split is an important exam point. Students sometimes wrongly assume that
using cloud means the provider handles all security. In IaaS, failing to patch a
guest operating system or leaving insecure ports open remains the customer’s
problem.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3 CAPABILITIES UNDER IaaS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

IaaS compute offerings include virtual machines of many sizes, sometimes bare-
metal instances, and groups of instances that can scale. Storage offerings include
block volumes attached to machines, object storage for files and backups, and
snapshots for recovery points. Networking offerings include virtual private
clouds, subnets, routing, gateways, load balancers, and VPN connections. Image
templates allow repeated creation of standardized machines, which is especially
useful in laboratories and automated environments.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.4 IMPORTANT FACTORS, BENEFITS, AND LIMITATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The decisive advantage of IaaS is flexibility. Almost any software stack that
runs on a conventional server can usually run on a virtual machine. This makes
IaaS excellent for lift-and-shift migration and for custom or legacy systems.
Another advantage is rapid provisioning compared with purchasing hardware.

The limitations follow from the same flexibility. Administrative burden remains
high. Operating-system hardening, patching, backup agents, and monitoring must be
handled thoughtfully. Cost can also rise if machines are oversized or left running
when idle. In multi-tenant environments, noisy neighbors and misconfiguration
risks require careful design.

Important selection factors therefore include skill availability in the team,
need for OS-level control, expected duration of workloads, and willingness to
operate security controls at the infrastructure layer.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.5 REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES AND USE CASES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Amazon EC2, Google Compute Engine, and Azure Virtual Machines are standard IaaS
examples. A student starting a Linux virtual machine for a CC-I practical is using
IaaS. A company moving an on-premises application server image into the cloud with
minimal code change is also using IaaS. Web hosting on virtual machines, disaster
recovery environments, and high-performance compute clusters are common use cases.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.6 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A complete IaaS answer includes definition, responsibility split, major
capabilities, advantages, limitations, examples, and at least one use case
explained in context.

4. PLATFORM AS A SERVICE (PaaS)
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1 DEFINITION AND CORE IDEA
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Platform as a Service provides a ready-made environment for developing, testing,
and deploying applications without forcing the customer to manage the underlying
servers and operating systems in detail. The provider supplies runtime, operating
system maintenance for the platform, middleware, and often deployment tooling.
The customer concentrates on writing and configuring application code and on the
data that the application uses.

PaaS sits between IaaS and SaaS in terms of control. It is less raw than
virtual machines and less finished than end-user business applications.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2 RESPONSIBILITY SPLIT
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Under PaaS, the provider typically manages hardware, virtualization, operating
system, and runtime platform. The customer manages application code, application-
level configuration, and data. User access to the application also remains a
customer concern. This division is why PaaS is popular with development teams:
much undifferentiated heavy lifting disappears, while product logic stays under
team control.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3 CAPABILITIES UNDER PaaS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A PaaS environment usually offers language and framework runtimes, managed
application hosting, deployment pipelines, and scaling features for application
instances. Many platforms also integrate managed databases, caching, messaging,
identity, and logging as nearby services. The developer’s daily experience becomes
“push code and configure services,” rather than “install packages on Linux and
tune web servers from scratch.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.4 IMPORTANT FACTORS, BENEFITS, AND LIMITATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The major benefit is speed. Teams can move from idea to deployed prototype
quickly, which is ideal for startups, hackathons, and academic projects. Platform
patching and much of the scaling machinery are handled for the customer. This
reduces operational distraction.

The limitations are reduced low-level control and potential platform constraints.
Unusual system libraries, kernel modules, or exotic networking setups may be
difficult or impossible. Heavy use of proprietary platform features can increase
vendor lock-in, making later migration harder. Debugging can also feel less
transparent because some internals are hidden.

Important factors when choosing PaaS include development speed, acceptable
platform constraints, lock-in tolerance, and whether the workload is a new
application rather than a deeply customized legacy system.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.5 REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES AND USE CASES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Google App Engine, AWS Elastic Beanstalk, Azure App Service, and Heroku-style
platforms are typical examples. A student deploying a Node.js or Python API on a
managed platform without manually administering Linux packages is using PaaS.
Web backends, mobile application backends, and microservice deployments are common
use cases.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.6 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Stress that PaaS serves developers and application deployment. Explicitly contrast
it with IaaS, where the customer still manages the operating system, and with
SaaS, where the customer mainly uses finished software.

5. SOFTWARE AS A SERVICE (SaaS)
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1 DEFINITION AND CORE IDEA
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Software as a Service delivers complete software applications over the Internet,
usually through a browser or mobile app, on a subscription or per-user basis.
The customer does not install and maintain the full application stack. Instead,
the provider operates the application and exposes it as a ready service. This is
the model most end users experience daily, often without using the word “cloud.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2 RESPONSIBILITY SPLIT
------------------------------------------------------------------------

In SaaS, the provider generally manages infrastructure, platform, and the
application itself, including updates and much of the operational security of the
service. The customer still manages user accounts, roles, acceptable use, and the
data entered into the system. Device security and phishing awareness also remain
customer-side concerns. The important factor is that “provider-managed software”
does not mean “no customer responsibility at all.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.3 CHARACTERISTICS OF SaaS DELIVERY
------------------------------------------------------------------------

SaaS applications are commonly multi-tenant, meaning many customer organizations
share the same application platform with logical separation. Updates are rolled
out centrally, so users receive new features without local reinstallation.
Billing is often predictable as monthly or yearly subscription per user, though
usage-based add-ons may exist. Integration with other systems typically happens
through APIs and connectors.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.4 IMPORTANT FACTORS, BENEFITS, AND LIMITATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The greatest benefit is ease of adoption. A college can enable institutional email
and document collaboration quickly without running mail servers. Maintenance
overhead is low compared with self-hosted equivalents. Access from multiple
devices supports mobility and remote learning.

Limitations include weaker deep customization, possible integration friction with
legacy systems, dependence on vendor uptime and roadmap, and concerns about data
residency or export. Internet dependency is especially visible in SaaS because
the application itself is remote. For highly specialized workflows, SaaS may be
too rigid unless the vendor offers strong configuration options.

Important selection factors are process standardization, compliance needs, vendor
trust, integration requirements, and total subscription cost at organizational
scale.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.5 REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES AND USE CASES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Gmail, Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Salesforce, Zoho CRM, Slack, Zoom, and
many learning platforms are SaaS examples. A college using Google Classroom
instead of hosting its own complete learning-management infrastructure is making
a SaaS choice. CRM for sales teams, HR portals, and accounting subscriptions are
classic business use cases.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.6 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Define SaaS carefully, explain multi-tenancy and subscription delivery, discuss
responsibility boundaries, and give at least three concrete product examples with
one institutional use case.


================================================================================
6. COMPARISON OF IaaS, PaaS, AND SaaS
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.1 RESPONSIBILITY COMPARISON IN WORDS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The cleanest way to compare the three models is to walk the technology stack.
Physical hardware and virtualization are provider-managed in all three popular
public-cloud cases. The operating system and runtime are customer-managed in IaaS,
but provider-managed in PaaS and SaaS. The application is customer-managed in
IaaS and PaaS, but provider-managed in SaaS. Data remains a customer
responsibility in all models, even when the provider stores it.

This is why IaaS feels like renting machines, PaaS feels like renting an app
platform, and SaaS feels like renting finished software.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.2 FEATURE COMPARISON TABLE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Aspect              | IaaS                  | PaaS                   | SaaS
--------------------|-----------------------|------------------------|--------------------
Primary consumer    | SysAdmin / DevOps     | Developers             | End users / business
Control             | Highest               | Medium                 | Lowest
Customization       | Very high             | Medium                 | Limited to settings
Ops burden          | High                  | Medium to low          | Lowest
Time to value       | Moderate              | Fast                   | Fastest
Typical billing     | VM/storage/network    | App platform usage     | Subscription / seats
Example             | EC2, GCE VMs          | App Engine, Beanstalk  | Gmail, Salesforce


------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.3 COMBINED REAL-LIFE SCENARIO
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A retail startup may use all three models together. It can adopt SaaS for email
and CRM, deploy its custom order API on PaaS, and run a specialized legacy
inventory tool on an IaaS virtual machine. This scenario is powerful in exams
because it shows that the models are complementary tools, not mutually exclusive
religions.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.4 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

For difference questions, combine a short prose explanation with a table and one
integrated example. Tables alone without explanation look incomplete; explanation
alone without structure looks messy.

7. OTHER CLOUD SERVICES BEYOND SPI
================================================================================

Modern cloud catalogs contain many specialized services often grouped under the
umbrella idea of Anything as a Service (XaaS). Unit 2 expects awareness of these
beyond the basic SPI trio.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.1 DATABASE AS A SERVICE (DBaaS)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Database as a Service offers managed database engines in which the provider
handles provisioning, patching, backups, and often replication options. The
customer focuses on schema design, queries, and data. The important factor is
reduced administrative load for a critical component. Real-life examples include
Amazon RDS, Cloud SQL, and Azure SQL Database. A student project that uses a
managed PostgreSQL instance instead of installing a database manually on a virtual
machine is consuming DBaaS.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.2 FUNCTION AS A SERVICE AND SERVERLESS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Function as a Service runs small units of code in response to events without
requiring the customer to manage servers. The platform scales executions
automatically and can scale to zero when idle. Billing follows invocations and
execution time. Important factors include cold-start behavior, execution limits,
and event-driven design style. AWS Lambda, Google Cloud Functions, and Azure
Functions are standard examples. Automatically generating a thumbnail when an
image enters cloud storage is a classic narrative.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.3 STORAGE AS A SERVICE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Storage as a Service provides durable storage over the network, commonly as
object storage, sometimes also as managed file or block services. Important
factors include storage class or tier, durability, access control, encryption,
and data egress charges. Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, and Azure Blob Storage
are widely known. Backing up large project videos or assignment archives to object
storage is a practical student example. Unit 4 develops storage more deeply.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.4 CONTAINER AS A SERVICE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Container as a Service provides managed environments for running containers, often
including managed Kubernetes offerings. Customers bring container images and
application definitions while the provider operates much of the control plane.
Important factors are portability of images and the remaining need to design
networking, scaling, and observability carefully. GKE, EKS, and AKS are common
examples and connect directly to syllabus practicals involving Docker on GCP.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.5 IDENTITY, DISASTER RECOVERY, AND SECURITY SERVICES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Identity as a Service centralizes authentication and single sign-on, often with
multi-factor authentication. Disaster Recovery as a Service and Backup as a
Service replicate or protect systems so that recovery objectives can be met after
failure. Security as a Service delivers capabilities such as email filtering or
managed detection tooling through cloud subscription. In all these cases, the
important factor is that specialized operational expertise is consumed as a
service rather than fully built in-house.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.6 HOW TO WRITE THIS IN EXAMS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

After defining XaaS, explain at least five services in short paragraphs with one
example each. Then note that real architectures usually combine them — for
example IaaS or PaaS with DBaaS, object storage, and serverless functions.

8. CLOUD DEPLOYMENT MODELS (TYPES OF CLOUD)
================================================================================

Deployment models, often called types of cloud in syllabus language, describe
where cloud infrastructure resides and who consumes it.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.1 PUBLIC CLOUD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A public cloud is owned and operated by a provider and made available to the
general public over the Internet. Resources are multi-tenant at the physical
layer, although each customer receives logically isolated accounts, networks, and
access controls. The important factors are low entry cost, vast on-demand
capacity, and reduced direct control over the physical facility.

Advantages include almost immediate access to global infrastructure and freedom
from data-center capital expenditure. Limitations include shared-environment
concerns for some regulated workloads and dependence on provider regions and
policies. AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud Platform are the standard
examples. Student free-tier labs are typically public-cloud experiences.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.2 PRIVATE CLOUD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A private cloud is used exclusively by a single organization. It may be built
on-premises or hosted by a third party, but access remains dedicated to that
organization. The important factors are control, customization, and cost. Private
clouds can enforce tailored security and compliance regimes, yet they usually
require significant investment and skilled staff, and they rarely match the raw
elastic scale of hyperscale public clouds.

Banks, defense organizations, and large enterprises with strict data-control needs
often prefer private cloud for sensitive cores. A university might also operate a
private cloud for specialized research workloads that must remain internal.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.3 HYBRID CLOUD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A hybrid cloud combines two or more distinct cloud infrastructures, commonly
private and public, that remain unique environments but are connected to enable
portability and orchestration of workloads. One famous pattern is cloud bursting:
run steady workload privately, then burst excess demand into public cloud during
peaks.

Hybrid design is attractive because sensitive systems can remain under tighter
control while elastic or customer-facing components use public capacity. The
important factors are integration complexity, identity consistency, network
latency between environments, and unified monitoring. A hospital may keep
electronic patient records in a private environment while hosting its public
appointment website and telemedicine scaling layer on public cloud.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.4 COMMUNITY CLOUD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A community cloud is shared by several organizations that have common concerns,
such as regulatory requirements, industry mission, or security standards. Cost
and governance are shared among members, and access is restricted to the
community. Government consortia or university research alliances sometimes adopt
this model. The important factors are shared purpose, collective governance, and
narrower ecosystem compared with global public clouds.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.5 COMPARISON AND SELECTION FACTORS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Model      | Who uses it             | Control | Cost character      | Typical fit
-----------|-------------------------|---------|---------------------|----------------------
Public     | General orgs/public     | Lower   | Low entry, OpEx     | Startups, labs, SaaS
Private    | One organization        | Higher  | High build/operate  | Sensitive enterprise
Hybrid     | Mixed environments      | Medium  | Mixed               | Regulated + elastic
Community  | Peer organizations      | Medium  | Shared              | Govt/edu/industry

Selection should consider data sensitivity, compliance, budget, need for extreme
elasticity, existing infrastructure investments, and the organization’s ability
to manage complexity.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.6 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Define all four types in paragraph form, include the comparison table, explain
cloud bursting under hybrid, and give one example each. This combination usually
covers full marks for deployment-model questions.

9. CLOUD ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
9.1 MEANING OF CLOUD ARCHITECTURE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cloud architecture is the organized structure of components through which cloud
services are delivered. It explains how user devices connect to applications and
how those applications rely on platforms, infrastructure, storage, management,
and security. A strong architecture answer is both descriptive and process-
oriented: it names the parts and explains how a request moves through them.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
9.2 FRONT END, NETWORK, AND BACK END
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The front end is the client side. It includes web browsers, mobile applications,
desktop clients, and developer tools such as command-line interfaces. This is
what people see and touch. The network path, usually the Internet and sometimes
private links, carries requests and responses. The back end is the provider side,
containing the application logic, service platforms, virtualized resources,
storage systems, and the management planes that keep everything operating.

Thinking in these three blocks prevents architecture answers from becoming a
random list of buzzwords.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
9.3 MAJOR COMPONENTS EXPLAINED
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Client infrastructure comprises the devices and local software used to access
cloud services. The application component is the software experience delivered to
the user, especially visible in SaaS. The service layer represents capabilities
offered by the cloud, such as compute, storage, messaging, or identity services.
Runtime provides the execution environment for application code, which is
especially important in PaaS. Storage preserves state through databases, object
stores, or file systems. Infrastructure supplies virtual machines, virtual
networks, and related foundations. Management covers provisioning, monitoring,
orchestration, and billing. Security spans identity, encryption, network
controls, and compliance tooling. Connectivity binds consumers to providers.

Each component matters because cloud is not only “an application on a server.” It
is a coordinated system of access, execution, persistence, control, and
protection.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
9.4 ARCHITECTURAL PROPERTIES AND REQUEST FLOW
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Good cloud architectures emphasize scalability, elasticity, high availability,
loose coupling through APIs, automation, observability, and multi-tenant
isolation. A typical request flow begins at the user device, passes DNS and load
balancing, reaches application services, interacts with caches or databases and
object storage, and returns a response while management systems record metrics
and costs.

An e-commerce illustration helps. Shoppers open a website, a load balancer spreads
traffic, application servers handle carts and orders, a database stores
transactions, object storage and CDN serve product images, autoscaling reacts to
sale-day peaks, and IAM with HTTPS protect access. That story is cloud
architecture in living form.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
9.5 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Draw front end, network, and back end. Explain seven to nine components in short
paragraphs, then narrate one end-to-end example. Diagrams attract marks when
accompanied by explanation.

10. CLOUD COMPUTING REFERENCE MODEL
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
10.1 PURPOSE OF A REFERENCE MODEL
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A cloud computing reference model is a conceptual framework that describes cloud
layers, service relationships, and actor roles in a standardized way. It is not
itself a product from one vendor. Its purpose is to give students, architects,
auditors, and providers a shared vocabulary. With a reference model, one can map
IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS onto layers and discuss responsibilities consistently even
when brand names differ.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
10.2 LAYERED VIEW EXPLAINED
------------------------------------------------------------------------

At the bottom sits the physical or fabric layer: servers, storage arrays, network
devices, and facilities. Above it lies the virtual infrastructure layer, where
hypervisors, virtual machines, virtual networks, and virtual storage create the
IaaS-facing view. The platform layer then adds operating-system abstractions,
runtimes, middleware, and deployment services associated with PaaS. The
application layer delivers SaaS-style software to end users. An access or
presentation layer includes portals, APIs, and client applications through which
consumers interact.

Across all layers run cross-cutting concerns: security, management and monitoring,
governance, and metering. These concerns are easy to forget in shallow answers,
yet they are essential because no layer is useful if it cannot be secured,
operated, and billed responsibly.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
10.3 ACTORS AND ROLES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Reference discussions often identify several roles. The cloud consumer uses
services. The cloud provider offers and operates them. A cloud broker may
intermediately manage use, performance, and delivery across multiple providers.
A cloud auditor performs independent assessments of security or compliance. A
cloud carrier provides network transport between consumer and provider. In many
exam answers, consumer and provider are essential; mentioning the additional roles
can earn enrichment marks when relevant.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
10.4 MAPPING TO SPI AND REAL LIFE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The mapping is direct: infrastructure layer corresponds mainly to IaaS, platform
layer to PaaS, and application layer to SaaS. Consider Gmail. The consumer is the
user; the provider is Google; the access layer is the browser or app; the
application layer is Gmail itself; lower layers remain hidden and provider-
managed. By contrast, when a student creates a virtual machine, the consumer
operates at the infrastructure layer and must manage the guest operating system
and applications above it.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
10.5 IMPORTANT FACTORS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A reference model is conceptual, so answers should not confuse it with one
company’s marketing diagram. Real systems instantiate these layers using concrete
services. The model helps decide where customization happens and where
responsibility sits. Security and management must be described as cross-cutting,
not as optional extras.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
10.6 EXAM TIP
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Define the reference model, draw the layered stack, map layers to IaaS/PaaS/SaaS,
mention cross-cutting security and management, and explain consumer/provider roles
with one example.

11. DECISION GUIDES — CHOOSING THE RIGHT MODEL
================================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------------------
11.1 CHOOSING A SERVICE MODEL
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Choose IaaS when operating-system control is necessary, when migrating legacy
servers with minimal change, or when specialized configurations cannot fit a
managed platform. Choose PaaS when the priority is rapid application development
and the team wants to avoid server administration. Choose SaaS when the need
matches a standard business or academic application and low maintenance matters
more than deep customization.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
11.2 CHOOSING A DEPLOYMENT MODEL
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Choose public cloud for agility and low upfront cost when workloads are allowed
to run there. Choose private cloud when exclusive control and specialized
compliance dominate, and when funding and skills exist. Choose hybrid when some
systems must stay controlled locally while others benefit from public elasticity.
Choose community cloud when several organizations share mission and governance
needs closely enough to operate together.

11.3 ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO
------------------------------------------------------------------------

A fintech startup may place its mobile backend on public cloud PaaS for speed,
use managed databases with strong encryption, and adopt SaaS tools for internal
email and CRM, while carefully aligning data practices to regulations. Explaining
such a scenario in an exam shows judgment, not only memory.

12. EXAM QUICK REFERENCE
================================================================================

Must-memorize definitions:
  Service model — classification by capability delivered and management split.
  Deployment model — classification by ownership/access (types of cloud).
  IaaS — rent virtual infrastructure.
  PaaS — rent application platform/runtime.
  SaaS — rent finished software over the Internet.
  Hybrid cloud — composition of private and public (or multiple distinct clouds)
  bound together for portability and orchestration.
  Cloud architecture — structural organization of cloud components.
  Reference model — conceptual layered framework of cloud services and roles.

Must-memorize lists:
  SPI service models; public/private/hybrid/community deployment models;
  examples of other services such as DBaaS, FaaS, CaaS, IDaaS, storage services;
  architecture components; reference-model roles.

Confusing pairs:
  Service model asks what you consume; deployment model asks where/who.
  PaaS means you build your app on a managed platform; SaaS means you use a
  finished app.
  Private cloud means exclusive infrastructure for one organization; a VPC is a
  logically isolated network inside a public cloud account.

14. FIVE-MINUTE RAPID REVISION
================================================================================

Unit 2 classifies cloud through service models, deployment models, architecture,
and a reference model. SPI means IaaS for infrastructure, PaaS for platforms, and
SaaS for software, with control falling and convenience rising as one moves up the
stack. Types of cloud are public, private, hybrid, and community. Hybrid often
supports cloud bursting. Beyond SPI, services such as DBaaS, FaaS, CaaS, and
managed storage are widely used. Architecture includes client, application,
service, runtime, storage, infrastructure, management, security, and connectivity.
The reference model layers physical, virtual infrastructure, platform, and
application views, with security and management across all layers. Always explain
responsibility split and give real examples in answers.
